#+TITLE: Aristotle was right | Embracing the dark side Part 2
#+FEATURED: blog/ai-crusades-pt2.jpg
#+DESCRIPTION: How far can AI go, and what would it take to prove Aristotle wrong?

@@html:<a href="/blog/2026-10-21-embracing-the-dark-side/">Part 1</a>@@ argued from Aristotle and Aquinas that a machine can't think, and can't improve itself into a mind. What I couldn't close was how far degree can go.

So here's how far it goes, what would prove Aristotle wrong, and how to read the AI companies in the meantime.

* What they claim
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: claim
:END:

Not that AI will stay bad at things. That was a bet on engineering, and I lost it in Part 1.

The claim is about what it is. Understanding isn't something matter does.[fn:p1agi] And nothing gives itself what it doesn't have, so a machine can improve itself in degree, never in kind.[fn:p1improve] Kind here means a nature, what a thing is, not a sort of tool.

No benchmark touches either one. Ace every test the AI companies can write, and it's still more of the same.

Some grant all of that, and say it was never in dispute. Nobody serious expects a mind to pop out of more compute, they say. The scores just keep climbing, past most of us already, and past all of us if the scaling holds. What's new won't be the machine. It'll be what happens to the world.

Then we agree. Degree without a ceiling, and never a mind, is the whole claim. And Part 1 already said the trouble comes first.[fn:p1first]

Not everybody stops there. The company that makes Claude calls it "a genuinely novel kind of entity in the world," and says, "We are not sure whether Claude is a moral patient."[fn:kind] A moral patient is something that can be wronged.

So it matters which. A tool's work belongs to the hand that moves it.[fn:p1hand] A mind's would be its own.

James Ross put the claim sharply, and everything else here hangs on his argument. "Some thinking (judgment) is determinate in a way no physical process can be." He builds on Saul Kripke's quus, a rule that agrees with plus whenever both numbers are under 57, and answers 5 otherwise.[fn:kripke] Whatever a circuit does, some rule like quus fits it as well as plus does, and which one it's doing is settled by whoever reads it. Then look at what it would answer for 68 plus 57, an engineer says. Kripke saw that coming. A circuit only ever gives finitely many answers, some of them wrong, and which ones count as wrong is settled by whoever designed it.[fn:dispo]

Then the training fixes it, says the next reply. A training objective is the same thing written down, a choice of what counts as right, and someone made it. The trouble was never that we can't tell which rule a circuit follows. Nothing in the circuit decides.

So "the machine cannot in principle add." It "adds, calculates, recalls, etc., by simulation." As he puts it, "The machine adds the way puppets walk."[fn:ross] Edward Feser has spent years defending that argument.[fn:feser13]

The obvious reply turns the puzzle around. Kripke's skeptic was asking about people, not machines. How do I know I add, and not quus?

Because the doubt has to mean exactly what it doubts. "To become convinced that I can only simulate the recognition that two Euclidean right triangles with equal sides are congruent, I have to judge negatively with all the determinateness that has just been denied." The rejoinder, Ross says, "defeats itself." And its price is everything. "We can do nothing logical at all, and no pure mathematics either. Now, who believes that?"[fn:rossself]

The meaning in the machine's answer is ours. We put it in, and we read it out.

* How far can it go
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: far
:END:

Aristotle never gives a number. He tells you what sort of far.

In kind, not one step. "One man cannot be more man than another, as that which is white may be more or less white than some other white object."[fn:cat5] Faster, sharper and cheaper come in degrees. What a thing is doesn't.

In degree, he sets no ceiling. He imagined the limit himself, in the /Politics/.[fn:pol14]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
For if every instrument could accomplish its own work, obeying or anticipating the will of others, like the statues of Daedalus, or the tripods of Hephaestus... if, in like manner, the shuttle would weave and the plectrum touch the lyre without a hand to guide them, chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves.
#+END_QUOTE

A shuttle that weaves by itself. A tool that anticipates your will. That's autocomplete, and that's an agent. It goes further than beating me at my job. It's the work of every servant there is.

He was writing about slaves. If tools could work by themselves, he says, nobody would need slaves. The machine is the instrument he could only imagine.

Aquinas names the machines outright. An arrow "goes straight towards the target, as though it were endowed with reason to direct its course. The same may be seen in the movements of clocks and all engines put together by the art of man." The order you see in the machine is the order of the mind that made it. Animals look prudent too, he says, "not because they reason or exercise any choice about things." The sign is that "all that share in one nature, invariably act in the same way."[fn:st13]

He even imagined a machine that builds itself. Aristotle said, "If the ship-building art were in the wood, it would produce the same results by nature."[fn:phys28] Aquinas took it further. Nature "is nothing but a certain kind of art, i.e., the divine art, impressed upon things... It is as if the shipbuilder were able to give to timbers that by which they would move themselves to take the form of a ship."[fn:inphys]

Machine learning is the nearest we've come to that shipbuilder. We don't shape the timbers. We give them a rule, and they shape themselves. It's still the shipbuilder's ship.[fn:p1form]

Its reach is borrowed, too. "The couch is not like the axe, but like the art which is in the craftsman's mind."[fn:st62] The machine's ceiling is our art and our will, multiplied.

And if it gets away from us? Aristotle has a word for what happens by itself, with nobody meaning it, /automaton/. "Thus the spontaneous is even according to its derivation the case in which the thing itself happens in vain. The stone that struck the man did not fall for the purpose of striking him." And "spontaneity and chance, therefore, are posterior to intelligence and nature."[fn:phys26]

So if it outruns the men who built it, that isn't a rebellion. It's an accident. The stone still kills.[fn:p1leash]

The believers have one passage on their side, and it's Aristotle's. "Nature proceeds little by little from things lifeless to animal life in such a way that it is impossible to determine the exact line of demarcation."[fn:hist81]

Aquinas answers it. "The lowest in the higher genus touches the highest of the lower species." An oyster is barely more than a plant, and still an animal.[fn:scg68] A line you can't see is still a line.

The same goes for general intelligence. A machine that can do one more sort of task, then a thousand more, is the same power reaching further. Grasping what a thing is was never one of the tasks.

At the top of its scale, the machine touches the bottom of ours. That's where it fools us.[fn:p1cog]

It can anticipate your will.

It can't have one.

* What would prove them wrong
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: wrong
:END:

No benchmark can do it. The claim is metaphysics, so what would refute it is an argument, not a demo.

There are two places to push. Show that Ross is wrong, that a circuit really adds, or that we don't either. Or show that a thing can give itself what it doesn't have, an effect bigger than all of its causes.

Few believers take the second road. Most say the data, the architecture and the compute account for everything the machine does, and on what it does, I agree. So the whole fight is on the first road, whether understanding is something matter does.

The second road is closed anyway. Feser's /Aristotle's Revenge/ argues that Aristotle's metaphysics, act and potency and the four causes, "is not only compatible with modern science, but is implicitly presupposed by modern science."[fn:revenge] A bug has a cause. A loss curve falls because something makes it fall. Every engineer who built these AI models leans on that every day.

If a machine really gave itself what it lacked, the effect would outrun its causes. And if Feser is right, the reasoning behind AI goes down with Aristotle.

So when the AI world seems to break Aristotle, there are two explanations. Either the foundations of all science are wrong, including the science AI is built on. Or we've missed a cause.

I know which one I'd bet on.

Is that dodging? No more than physics does. Feser points out that some of science's own key claims "appear to be unfalsifiable," like the conservation of energy and the second law of thermodynamics. "These are so general and so fundamental to the picture of the world presented by modern science that any apparent counterexample would be taken to be evidence, not that these principles are false, but rather that revision is called for somewhere else in the body of scientific theory."[fn:unfals]

Hand a physicist a perpetual motion machine, and he looks for the hidden battery.[fn:pauli]

What about a test? The cleanest anyone has proposed is Demis Hassabis's. Train a model on everything known before 1911, and see if it comes up with general relativity on its own, the way Einstein did.[fn:ball] Not led there by questions that already hold the answer. On its own.

Nobody has passed it. Nobody has claimed to.

If a model ever does, it won't refute Aristotle. Its causes will still be the data, the architecture and the men who built it.[fn:p1surprise]

Einstein had causes too, the physics before him and the brain he was born with. What he did with them was understand. Whether a machine does that is the first road again, and no test settles it.

But a pass will be the strongest pressure Aristotle has faced. And it will show how far degree can go.

* Couldn't we give it one?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: give
:END:

Grant that nothing gives itself what it doesn't have. The machine doesn't have to. We have minds, and we built it. Part 1 even quoted Wolfgang Smith saying something new can come from "the free act of an intelligent agent."[fn:p1smith] What's an engineer, if not that?

And nature has done it all along. Chemistry became life, life became animals, and animals, on most accounts, became us, one horizontal cause after another. So either the principle forbids every new kind in nature, which is absurd, or it allows them, and then why not a mind in a machine?

That's fair to Part 1's wording, read loosely. "Never in kind" sounds like no new kind of anything, ever. Aquinas never said that. For the things we make, it holds, since art only adds accidents. Nature, on most accounts, brings forth new kinds. The line he never moves is the mind.[fn:p1one]

Start with the things we make. A compiler isn't an adder, and a learning algorithm is neither. Their forms are real, and Aristotle is the last man who'd say it's all just atoms. But they're forms of art. "In artifacts the matter alone is held to be substance, whereas the forms of artifacts are accidents," Aquinas says, and art "operates only on that which is already constituted in existence by nature."[fn:art] A model can rewrite itself into a new sort of tool forever. It's still the machine, arranged.

Then nature. New species, Aquinas says, "if any such appear, existed beforehand in various active powers."[fn:st73] Whether the theory of evolution fits that, Thomists still argue. Either way, the line holds at the mind. The rational soul, he says, "is not educed from the potentiality of matter."[fn:st90a2] The Philosopher said it first. "It remains, then, for the reason alone so to enter and alone to be divine, for no bodily activity has any connexion with the activity of reason."[fn:ga] The body comes from the parents. Reason comes from outside.

Animals are a different case. Their souls, he says, are "naturally brought into existence by certain corporeal agents that reduce the matter from potentiality to act."[fn:st118a1] So a dog's knack for a problem is within matter's reach, and the machine may match it. The line is at understanding, not at cleverness.

Grant evolution everything it claims, and it gets you a body. It can't get you a mind, any more than the engineer can.

Is "God makes the mind" just a way to make sure I can't lose? No. It stands on the argument that the mind isn't matter, Part 1's triangle and Ross's quus. Refute those, and this falls with them.

Couldn't God give a machine a mind, the way he gives one to every child? Then God made it, not the engineer, and not the scale.[fn:st76]

Now the minds we have. A father has one, and so does his son, and the father really is a cause of the son. He still doesn't make the mind. "Man begets his like," Aquinas says, "forasmuch as by his seminal power the matter is disposed." That's as far as a body's power reaches, since "it is impossible for an active power existing in matter to extend its action to the production of an immaterial effect." The body comes by way of the parents. "The intellectual soul is from God alone."[fn:st118]

Having a mind isn't enough to give one. A creature acts on what's already there, by changing it, and a mind isn't made out of anything. "God alone can create... everything else acts by producing a change."[fn:st90]

A father begets his son and still can't make his son's mind.

The engineer isn't even a father.

* When not to believe them
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: believe
:END:

If Aristotle is right, an AI that seems to have a mind is first a question about who says so. Motives don't settle the question. Arguments do. This is only how far to trust what you're told.

Aristotle gives three reasons a man tells you something false. He's mistaken, "through want of good sense." He knows better, but "because of their moral badness do not say what they really think." Or he's honest, "but not well disposed to their hearers."[fn:rhet] Only one of them is lying.

The motive is money. OpenAI ran a $20.9 billion operating loss last year.[fn:openai] Anthropic put nearly $9 billion into infrastructure in the first half of this year alone, and its computing commitments could reach $750 billion.[fn:anthropic] An AI company spending like that needs the next round of money, and the next round needs a mind to be just around the corner.

Watch for something new that was already there. In October 2025 an OpenAI executive posted that GPT-5 had "found solutions to 10 (!) previously unsolved Erdős problems." It had found papers that already solved them. Hassabis called it "embarrassing."[fn:erdos]

The real ones came later. AI has since solved some Erdős problems for good, and Claude found a construction Donald Knuth had chased for weeks.[fn:real] Believe those. They're still degree.

Watch for something new that's a measuring trick. Abilities that seemed to appear out of nowhere at a certain size mostly "evaporate with different metrics or with better statistics."[fn:mirage]

Watch the word move with the money. OpenAI's contract with Microsoft once defined AGI by profits. By April 2026 OpenAI's payments to Microsoft were to continue "independent of OpenAI's technology progress."[fn:agi] That's a contract term, not a metaphysics.

And trust the claim that costs them. Anthropic wants the Pentagon's business, and its models were already running across the Department of War. It still told the Pentagon this year that "frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons," and lost government business over it.[fn:dow] Its own researchers found Claude 3.7 Sonnet's written reasoning mentioned a hint that changed its answer only "25% of the time."[fn:cot] That finding cuts against the product, so it's likely true.

Anthropic also reported Claude's "emergent introspective awareness." It noticed a thought planted in its own workings "about 20% of the time."[fn:introspect] The paper's title says awareness. The number says one time in five. Believe the number. So do the authors, who call it "highly unreliable and limited in scope."

Their doubt about whether Claude is a moral patient costs them too, and it's honest. It's a question, not a claim, and they're right to ask it. This post is my answer.

Their business bets the other way. They talk like it's a mind,[fn:p1grace] and sell it by the token. Believe the sale.

If it were a mind, that would be a slave trade.

Aristotle's instrument was supposed to free the slave, not become one.

* The likely world
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: npc
:END:

So the world to expect isn't the one where the machine wakes up. It's the one Part 1 called.[fn:p1stood]

AI isn't going to become human.

Humans are going to become NPCs.

We can already measure the start of it. In one study, people writing eight-sentence stories could ask an AI for ideas. Their stories were judged more creative, and also "more similar to each other than stories by humans alone."[fn:doshi] When AI hands everybody the idea, everybody's story drifts toward the same place.

The danger was never a machine with a mind. It's men who stop using theirs, because a company let them think the machine might have one.[fn:p1code]

* A note on evolution
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evolution
:END:

This post doesn't ask you to decide on evolution, and that's on purpose. Its argument, in @@html:<a href="#give">Couldn't we give it one?</a>@@, is about the mind, and it holds whichever way you go on bodies. If it hung on evolution, readers on one side would stop at the door, and the question this post is about would get lost in a fight it doesn't need.

I haven't settled it myself, and the Church never has. No council ever defined how bodies came to be. The Fathers didn't even settle how each soul does. Augustine put it to Jerome, "Teach me, I beseech you, what I may teach to others, teach me what I ought to hold as my own opinion."[fn:aug166] What the early catechisms hand on is the soul as God's own work. "Know also that thou hast a soul self-governed, the noblest work of God, made after the image of its Creator," Cyril of Jerusalem told the people he was preparing for baptism.[fn:cyril]

Rome has kept it open in our own time. Pius XII allowed research and discussion about evolution "in as far as it inquires into the origin of the human body as coming from pre-existent and living matter - for the Catholic faith obliges us to hold that souls are immediately created by God." He asked that the reasons for both opinions, "those favorable and those unfavorable to evolution, be weighed and judged with the necessary seriousness, moderation and measure."[fn:hg36]

So there are three honest places to stand.

You can hold that bodies evolved and every soul comes from God. Many Thomists do, and so did John Paul II. He called evolution "more than a hypothesis," and in the same message drew the line where Pius did. Theories that "consider the spirit as emerging from the forces of living matter or as a mere epiphenomenon of this matter, are incompatible with the truth about man." With man, he said, there's "an ontological leap."[fn:jp2]

You can hold that bodies didn't evolve. Wolfgang Smith, whom Part 1 leans on, wrote a whole book against Teilhard de Chardin's evolution, and Fr. Chad Ripperger argues from first principles against macroevolution.[fn:critics]

Or you can weigh the reasons and wait, as Pius asked. That's where I am.

All three can sign what this post says. Grant evolution everything it claims about bodies, and you still don't get a mind.

What none of them can sign is evolutionism, the belief that mind itself rises out of matter once there's enough of it, arranged well enough. Teilhard made that a law of the universe. Rome's Holy Office warned in 1962 that his works "abound in such ambiguities and indeed even serious errors, as to offend Catholic doctrine." It said so while "prescinding from a judgement about those points that concern the positive sciences."[fn:monitum] It left the science alone and judged the philosophy.

That philosophy is the one this post argues against. Swap living matter for parameters, and it's the believers' pitch.

Whatever made the body, the mind was given.
