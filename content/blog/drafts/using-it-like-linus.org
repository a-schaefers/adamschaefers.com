#+TITLE: Using it like Linus
#+DESCRIPTION: What the machine did on real work, the rule I use it by, where I broke it in my own code, and what it means to pay for it.

In March I told you not to lean on AI to code. I changed my mind. This is the long account, for working programmers and the people who lead them.

* What it did

I built a whole platform by myself, [[https://merecatholicity.com/][Mere Catholicity]]. A library of old public-domain books, re-typeset. An AI research librarian that answers from that library, with citations. And a community with profiles and walls like Facebook, encrypted messages, anonymous accounts and calls.

Under the hood, it's no toy. A typed front end, its rules in PureScript. My own API on Cloudflare Workers, with a database, file storage and real-time chat. Every change goes through CI, waits for my approval before it reaches production, rolls out in stages, and gets tested against production every night.

It took a week to get it standing, and a couple of months to build all of it.

I can give you the day, because [[https://github.com/merecatholicity/merecatholicity.com][the commit log]] kept it. On the seventh morning, July 23, the site was a book, some papers and a shelf of old texts. At twenty to ten, a comment box went in under seven of its pages. By half past two it had grown into a forum with thirteen rooms, and the whole thing had been through a security pass. About twelve hours after that, it had profiles, avatars and private messages.

It wasn't a one-off. My own Linux distro and an init system written in Emacs Lisp each took a week or two to stand up. I'd been a package maintainer on KISS Linux, and I'd already booted a distro with Emacs as its init once, by hand. On those, the machine flew where I already knew the ground.

It isn't magic. My own Vulkan driver for AMD cards got to the GPU in a week, and it's still stuck on its first triangle.

And it isn't only me. In its first five weeks, [[https://github.com/storytold/photocraft][PhotoCraft]], an open-source rebuild of Photoshop in Rust, with Claude among its co-authors, got to where it opens and re-saves 307 of the 309 Photoshop files in a standard test set. Its own README says it isn't ready for daily professional work yet. If I were Adobe, I'd be nervous anyway.

All of that is built from nothing, and most of the job is keeping somebody else's system running. That's the harder test, and I haven't run it yet. I expect the machine to pass that one too.

On the work I've given it, it's better than most coders I've worked with, and better than me. Flat out. I'm not convinced it's better than the best of us. It doesn't have to be. And it doesn't need a mind to do it. It guesses, but with a compiler, tests and a human review behind it, the guesses are good enough to count on.

* Even Linus

Linus Torvalds uses it. Tom's Hardware reported on [[https://www.tomshardware.com/software/linux/linus-torvalds-posts-his-first-llm-assisted-patch-to-the-linux-kernel-after-issuing-clear-pro-ai-directives-the-bossman-makes-use-of-ai-tooling-himself][a patch series he built with an LLM]], a branch to make reading files from the page cache scale. [[https://git.kernel.org/pub/scm/linux/kernel/git/torvalds/linux.git/commit/?id=4c0f3697118eb3a708ea79175f7f07c43339bfab][His write-up]] says how it was made.

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
The code, the test programs, the review behind the list above and the first draft of this text were done with an LLM coding assistant (Claude Code) under my direction. The measurements were run on my machine.
#+END_QUOTE

The write-up owns up to ten known problems. Another kernel developer, Kiryl Shutsemau, found the first version could get processes killed when a machine ran short of memory. Linus [[https://lore.kernel.org/all/CAHk-=wjLjgfwgSZ1RDk4YdNFcV0ntosMO5AmBoHD76Zb3HFY7Q@mail.gmail.com/][reworked it]] to hold back only the pages that get read again and again, with [[https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/torvalds_claude_code_page_cache_folio_patches_2026.html][two patches from Kiryl]]. The branch has since come down from his tree, and as of early November none of it has merged. That's peer review doing its job. Linus can use the tool and still own the result, with reviewers like Kiryl reading behind him, and few of us will ever get that much review.

Each patch carries an "Assisted-by: LLM" line, right next to his "Signed-off-by." A tool acts only as the hand moves it, so the work is the hand's. When Linus signs his patch, he owns it. When a company ships slop, it can't blame the model.

* The whiteboard

Most of us aren't Linus. That's what tests, review and staged rollouts are for. They hold what one head can't.

And Mitchell Hashimoto has [[https://x.com/mitchellh/status/2100249348345057389][a bar the rest of us can reach]]. You don't have to know the code line by line. But somebody should be able to pull you up to a whiteboard and have you explain what you shipped. Why X and not Y. Which data structure, and why. What happens when someone acts maliciously. Where it fails.

If you can't, you don't own it.

* The two roads

"Men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre," [[https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html][Aristotle says]]. Programmers become programmers by programming. [[https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2053.htm#article3][Aquinas says why it slips away]], that "the intellectual habit is diminished or even wholly destroyed by cessation from act." Watching the machine isn't doing the acts. And you can't answer for work you can't judge, or judge what you've stopped doing.

My platform is only partly on ground I knew. Its pipeline, its Terraform and its staged rollouts are my old trade, and I can judge that work on sight. The front end is another matter. Its rules are written in PureScript, and I had never written a line of PureScript. The machine wrote them anyway.

The hand work isn't what lets the machine build. It can build on ground you've never walked. The hand work is what lets you judge what it built. Where I knew the ground, I could tell good from bad at a glance. Where I didn't, there were two roads. Learn it well enough to check, or sign for work I couldn't read. The second road ends in code nobody understands, one approval at a time.

Anthropic [[https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills][tested the two roads]]. Developers learning a new library with an AI assistant finished about as fast as those coding by hand, then scored 50% on a quiz about it, against 67%, and the widest gap was on debugging. The ones who held up used it to understand, asking why, not just for code.

I got to choose. I took some of each. Parts of the PureScript I can follow now. For the rest I lean on the compiler, the tests and the pipeline, and that's signing for work I can't fully read.

* The hole in my own code

Then the whiteboard question came back to me. Claude was reviewing a draft of mine, and it read me one of Hashimoto's questions. What happens when someone acts maliciously? The platform said its messages were end-to-end encrypted, and I'd said so in print too. So I had the machine read that code and tell me what a crooked server could do.

It took minutes. The messages are sealed in the browser, and the server stores only ciphertext. That part holds. But every signed-in request also hands the server the account key, the secret your encryption keys are made from. The server keeps only a hash of it, and it still sees the key go by. An honest server can't read your messages. A crooked one, or one that's been broken into, could. That isn't what end-to-end means, and my own privacy page said "We cannot read them."

Nobody outside has reviewed that code, no cryptographer and no firm. And it isn't in the PureScript. It's a few hundred lines of TypeScript on a well-known library, so the hole wasn't hiding in the language I couldn't read. The types checked. The tests passed. The pipeline was green. None of them asks what a crooked server can do. That's a question about the design, and it doesn't get asked until it's somebody's job to ask it.

The machine could have told me in July, if I'd asked it that. In September I did ask one for a full review of the platform. It called the codebase disciplined, found other trouble, and never mentioned this. A broad question got a broad answer. And I can't tell you a man thought to ask the pointed one. A machine did, reading my own draft back to me, and the question was Hashimoto's before it was mine. What I can tell you is that I had it written down, and putting it to my own code had been my job since July.

So here's what I can defend today. The messages are encrypted in the browser and stored that way. Here's what I can't. That the house, whoever runs the server, couldn't read them if it turned on you. The same reading turned up other gaps of that kind, and I haven't run them all down.

Keeping the account key off the wire wouldn't make the house blind, since it still serves the code your browser runs. That's the ceiling for encryption that arrives in a web page, and mine was well under it.

So the claim comes down, on the site too, with this post. I say encrypted now, not end-to-end. The words go back up when the design is fixed and somebody who isn't me or a machine has reviewed it, and not before. Until then, treat those messages as private from the other members at best, and not from the house.

I wrote that the second road ends in code nobody understands. I'd already taken a few steps down it.

* When the job says use it

Maybe you don't get to choose. In March I also told you to tell your AI-pushing boss to get wrecked, and that's easy to say when you work for yourself. If the job says use it, and the deadline leaves no hour to read what it wrote, most of this post is advice for somebody freer than you. But the two roads are still there inside the job. You can't refuse the tool. You can still ask it why, the way the ones who held up did. You can still say out loud which parts you've read and which you're signing blind, so the risk sits with whoever set the deadline and isn't buried in your approval. Put it in the change itself, the way Linus does, with an Assisted-by line and a note on which of the whiteboard questions you can't answer yet, so your manager reads it before anyone signs off. And you can keep one thing you build by hand on your own time, the way a man with a desk job keeps his legs by walking. That's less than I'd want for you.

Coding is an art, and whether and how to use the machine is prudence. Losing the art isn't a sin in itself, but neglecting it when other people depend on what you understand can be.

* Why not stop it?

In March I'd have signed [[https://www.pauseai-us.org/petition/][the petition]] for "a global AI Treaty to ban superintelligent AI systems until they can be made safe." I wouldn't now. Men made the thing and men can stop it, but all of them would have to, and can isn't will.

A treaty has to hold. We tried one at the start of a race once. In 1946 the United States proposed putting atomic energy under international control, [[https://history.state.gov/milestones/1945-1952/baruch-plans][the Baruch Plan]]. The Soviets were three years from their own bomb, and it was dead at the UN by early 1947. This race has barely started, and every side thinks the prize is everything. I don't think a treaty would hold now either, and a treaty only the honest keep doesn't stop the race.

Nobody knows how far the machine's power can go. If it goes far enough to outrun the men holding the leash, I'll have been wrong about the pause, and everyone pays for it, not just me.

* Paying for it, and selling it

I pay for an AI subscription, and the company behind it is in that race. Doesn't my fee feed it? The evil isn't building the thing. It's the racing. My fee can't pay for the building without paying for the race too, since money is fungible. But what I buy is a tool, and the racing is their choice, not mine.

The moral theologians have a name for it, cooperation. Sharing in the evil, wanting what the other man wants, is formal cooperation, and it's never allowed. Helping without wanting it, from far off, is remote material cooperation, and it's allowed for a proportionate reason. The distinction in that form is St. Alphonsus Liguori's, from his /Theologia Moralis/, and its root is [[https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3064.htm#article7][Aquinas's double effect]]. I don't want the race, a monthly fee is a long way from it, and my livelihood is a proportionate reason. If using the tool ever meant wanting the race, I'd be out.

And it isn't only a fee. I sell [[https://github.com/a-schaefers/backseat-driver][Backseat Driver]], a tutor that runs on Claude Code. Every copy I sell sends the racer a customer, and my business does better when his does. That's closer than a subscription, so my reason has to be better. Mine is the case I just made, keeping programmers coding. That's also what a tutor's seller would say, so here's my test. If the people who use it can't stand at that whiteboard and explain their code without it, I'll pull it. And once an open model on people's own machines can tutor as well, I'll make it run there, so nobody needs the racer to keep their craft.

There's one more condition, scandal, which means not leading anyone else into sin by your example. Writing this makes me an example, so here are the terms, and I don't always meet them. I use it the way Linus does, I keep the craft, and I'd rather run my own model than rent one.

* Owning it

[[https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/62680/pg62680-images.html#page_47][Chesterton's fight]] was over who owns the means of making things. "Too much capitalism does not mean too many capitalists, but too few capitalists." A monthly bill to a lab is too few capitalists. We traded owning for renting with every managed service, and this is that trade at its worst.

He even had a case like ours. In his day it was electricity. A central plant made men depend on it, he granted. But cheap enough, it would give the small workshop its chance, and he'd use it to break up concentrated capital.

The models are the electricity this time. Friends of mine already run open models on hardware they own. One of them runs four agents at once on a single graphics card. The models aren't the frontier yet, but they do real work. Make them cheaper still, and a town could own a datacenter the way it owns its library, a shop could own its model, and the common man gets his workshop back.

America's founders protected a machine once already. The First Amendment guards "the freedom of speech, or of the press," and the press was a machine. Nobody needs a license to run one. A model on your own hardware is the press of our day, and nobody should need a license to run that either.

Stallman got there long before me. He has a name for renting what you could run yourself, [[https://www.gnu.org/philosophy/who-does-that-server-really-serve.html][Service as a Software Substitute]], and says it gives "someone else power over your computing." He'd tell me to cancel my subscription, and he'd have a point.

So why am I still renting? Because the open models can't yet do the work I make my living on. That reason has an end date.

*The day a model of my own does the job, I cancel.*
