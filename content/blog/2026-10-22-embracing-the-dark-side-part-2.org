#+TITLE: What would prove Aristotle wrong | Embracing the dark side Part 2
#+DESCRIPTION: Part 1 said a machine can't think, and can't improve itself into a mind. What would I have to see to call Aristotle and Aquinas wrong? And if they're right, how do I know when the labs are misleading me?

@@html:<a href="/blog/2026-10-21-embracing-the-dark-side/">Part 1</a>@@ asked two questions. Can a machine think? Can it improve itself into a mind? Aristotle and Aquinas say no.

Part 2 asks one. What would I have to see to say they're wrong?

And if they're right, how do I know when the labs are lying to me?

* What they claim
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: claim
:END:

Not that the machine will stay bad at things. That was a bet on engineering, and I lost it in Part 1.

The claim is about what it is. Two claims, really.

Understanding isn't something matter does.

Nothing gives itself what it doesn't have. A machine can improve itself in degree, never in kind.

No score touches either one. A higher score is more of the same.

James Ross put the first one sharply: "Some thinking (judgment) is determinate in a way no physical process can be." When you add, you add. A circuit only does what's consistent with adding, and with endless other rules that agree with adding so far. Which one it's doing is settled by whoever reads it. So, "the machine cannot in principle add." It "adds, calculates, recalls, etc., by simulation."[fn:ross] Edward Feser has spent years defending that argument.[fn:feser13]

The meaning in the machine's answer is ours. We put it in, and we read it out.

[fn:ross] James Ross, [[https://newdualism.org/papers/J.Ross/Ross-immateriality.htm][Immaterial Aspects of Thought]], /The Journal of Philosophy/ 89, no. 3 (1992).
[fn:feser13] Edward Feser, [[https://www.pdcnet.org/acpq/content/acpq_2013_0087_0001_0001_0032][Kripke, Ross, and the Immaterial Aspects of Thought]], /American Catholic Philosophical Quarterly/ 87, no. 1 (2013).

* What would prove them wrong
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: wrong
:END:

So no output can do it. It would take something deeper. You'd have to see an effect bigger than all of its causes.

A machine that gives itself what it doesn't have. Feed it only on itself, no new data, no measure set by a man, and have it come out understanding what it didn't before.

The cleanest test anyone has proposed is Demis Hassabis's. Train a model on everything known before 1911, and see if it comes up with general relativity on its own, the way Einstein did.[fn:ball] Not led there by questions that already hold the answer. On its own.

Nobody has passed it. Nobody has claimed to.

But look at what passing would cost.

Feser's book /Aristotle's Revenge/ argues that act and potency, form and cause are "not only compatible with modern science, but are implicitly presupposed by modern science."[fn:revenge] Every engineer who built these models leaned on them. A bug has a cause. A loss curve falls because something makes it fall. The scientist trusts that his instruments measure what's there, and that his own mind can know it.

If a machine really gave itself what it lacked, the effect would outrun its causes. Then the reasoning that built the machine goes down with Aristotle. Science doesn't get to keep its foundations while the machine kicks them out.

So when the world seems to break Aristotle, there are two explanations. Either the foundations of all science are wrong, including the science that built the machine. Or the report is wrong.

I know which one I'd bet on.

[fn:ball] Philip Ball, [[https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02804-x][in /Nature/]], 2026. Hassabis proposed it at the India AI Summit in February. A team including Alec Radford, who built the first GPT, has [[https://talkie-lm.com/introducing-talkie][a model trained on text from before 1931]] and lists the question among its goals.
[fn:revenge] Edward Feser, [[https://www.editiones-scholasticae.de/artikel/aristotles-revenge/][Aristotle's Revenge: The Metaphysical Foundations of Physical and Biological Science]] (2019), from the publisher's description.

* When they're lying
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lying
:END:

If you hold the philosophy, a claimed miracle is first a question about the claim.

Aristotle gives three reasons a man tells you something false. He's mistaken, "through want of good sense." He knows better, but "because of their moral badness do not say what they really think." Or he's honest, "but not well disposed to their hearers."[fn:rhet]

The motive is the economy. Part 1 has the numbers, losses in the tens of billions and commitments in the hundreds of billions. A lab spending like that needs the next round of money, and the next round needs a mind to be just around the corner.

So here's what to watch for.

Something new that was already there. In October 2025 an OpenAI executive posted that GPT-5 had "found solutions to 10 (!) previously unsolved Erdős problems." It had found papers that already solved them. Hassabis called it "embarrassing."[fn:erdos]

Something new that's a measuring trick. Abilities that seemed to appear out of nowhere at a certain size mostly "evaporate with different metrics or with better statistics."[fn:mirage]

A word that moves with the money. OpenAI's contract with Microsoft once defined AGI by profits, reportedly around $100 billion. Then an expert panel was to verify it. Then in April 2026 the payments were made to continue "independent of OpenAI's technology progress."[fn:agi] That's a contract term, not a metaphysics.

Mind talk and tool sales. If a lab truly believed its model was somebody, it couldn't rent him out by the token. Believe the sale.

And trust the claim that costs them. Anthropic told the Pentagon this year that "frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons," and lost government business over it.[fn:dow] Its own researchers found Claude's written reasoning mentioned a hint that changed its answer only "25% of the time,"[fn:cot] and that it noticed a thought planted in its own workings "about 20% of the time."[fn:introspect] Those findings cut against the product, so they're likely true. And they describe exactly what Aristotle would expect. Nobody home.

* The likely world
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: npc
:END:

So the world to expect isn't the one where the machine wakes up. It's the one Part 1 called.

AI isn't going to become human.

Humans are going to become NPCs.

That one we can already measure. In one study, stories written with ideas from AI were judged more creative, and also "more similar to each other than stories by humans alone."[fn:doshi] Everybody copying the same model, and wanting what its makers want for them.

The danger was never a machine with a mind. It's men who stop using theirs, because a company told them the machine has one.

Could I be wrong? Sure. Now you know what it would take.
