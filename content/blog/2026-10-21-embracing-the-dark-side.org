#+TITLE: Embracing the dark side
#+FEATURED: blog/ai-crusades.jpg
#+DESCRIPTION: Can a machine think? Can it improve itself into a mind? Aristotle and Aquinas say no. It doesn't need to. A programmer's case for using it anyway, and on whose terms.

In March I made a video telling you to "tell your AI-pushing boss to get
wrecked."[fn:artisan]

Here's what happened since: where I stood, what held up, what didn't, and
where I stand now.

When Aristotle broke with his own teacher, he called it a duty, "for the sake
of maintaining the truth even to destroy what touches us closely."[fn:truth]
What touches me closely is six essays and a video with my name on them.

AI is new, but the questions it raises are old, so I've gone back to Aristotle
and Aquinas to work through them.

1. [[#said][What I've said so far]] - six pieces this year against leaning on AI to code.
2. [[#perspectives][Perspectives]] - colleagues and friends, from "it changes
   everything" to "I won't touch it".
3. [[#stood][Where I stood]] - it imitates, it doesn't think, and that's already
   enough to wreck us.

     wouldn't live in it.
   - [[#crossroads][The crossroads]] - decentralize it the American way, or join

* What I've said so far
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: said
:END:

Here are the six, all from the Enchant Games journal.

- /The problem of the human-computer interface/.[fn:j1] LLMs are
  probability-based guessing machines, and English is too ambiguous to command
  a computer. The more precision a project needs, the closer you get to a real
  programming language again, and the less you need the LLM. I called it a
  borrow now, pay later approach, and I said the danger isn't a sentient
  machine. It's a human who has become an NPC.
- /Dijkstra was right/.[fn:j2] It turns out Dijkstra made the case long ago, in
  /On the foolishness of "natural language programming"/.[fn:ewd] A natural
  language interface means more work, not less.
- /The fall of the software engineer, the rise of the software artisan/.[fn:j3]
  Companies mandating AI turn engineers into robot babysitters. AI code is mid,
  because it's trained on code that is mostly mid. "We need less software
  engineers. We need more software artisans."
- /On the progressive surrender of technical autonomy/.[fn:j4] First they took
  our operating systems, then our editors, then our stacks. Now they want our
  brains. "We built machines to obey us. Now we ask them to think instead of
  us."
- /For the love of the game/.[fn:j5] Software was bloated long before LLMs, and
  AI only amplifies it. Programmers tolerate complexity because they don't love
  the game enough. The cure is the Unix philosophy, the classics, small
  programs, and knowing your system.
- /It's not all about speed/.[fn:j6] If it takes me longer but I grok it, that
  matters. "It's about ownership of understanding."

I still stand behind the heart of every one of them. Not every line.

* Perspectives
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: perspectives
:END:

I know a lot of people in software, colleagues and friends of every kind, and
they see AI every which way. Here's the best of what I heard.

"It changes everything."

That case came with homework, essays like /Machines of Loving Grace/, sent to
correct my intuition. Within 25 years the world won't be recognizable, and my
gut just hasn't caught up. The machine already out-designs the people making
this case, and agents already do part of the labs' own research. Let a thing
like that improve itself and you get something new on the earth, a threat to
liberty, maybe past stopping already. The hope is the researchers taking the
models apart to read their wiring, so at least somebody knows what's in there.

And it doesn't end in slop. Software is about to be exceptionally well
designed, better than we ever bothered to make it.

I read the essays. It's not a world I want to live in, and I say why in
[[#grace][So-called Machines of Loving Grace]].

"It's FUD."

The world runs on energy, and intelligence has diminishing returns. A genius
can't think his way to more power plants. Past a point, IQ stops turning into
power.

It's a good argument, until you ask who owns the power plants. Energy and
capital have owners, and you aren't one of them.

"The labs are wasting compute."

Open models on about $15,000 of hardware, running side by side, do most of the
same work, and there's a benchmark in the works to prove it. Four agents can
run at once on a single RTX 4090.

If that holds, it's the best news in this post. Everything I hope for in
[[#crossroads][The crossroads]] hangs on AI you can run at home.

"We were doomed already."

Anti-intellectualism did us in before any of this, and AI is just the
accelerant. A people that quit reading will hand its thinking to a chatbot
without a fight. Funny thing, that one came from the man running four agents
on his 4090.

I can't argue with it. It's why I land on [[#creed][church and the trivium]].

"I won't touch it."

They didn't sign up to pay to code, and they'd sooner change trades than
compromise.

There's some truth in every one of them.

* Where I stood
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: stood
:END:

The first camp had a point. I built an app by myself with the features of
Facebook and then some. Calls. Encrypted messages like Signal. Disappearing
messages like Snapchat. Anonymous accounts like 4chan.

It took a week.

That's when I knew software engineers were cooked.

But I still saw the philosophical problem. The machine can't have a thought of
its own. It apes what it absorbs and hands it back. It doesn't reason. It
imitates reasoning, and man is "the most imitative of living
creatures."[fn:poetics]

René Girard added what Aristotle left out.[fn:girard] We don't only copy each
other's ways. We copy each other's wants. So we're easy to fool, and easier
still to steer.

Most of the time, the imitation passes. We've tolerated mediocre software for
decades. It works, ship it. Now every other trade gets the same deal.

The machine is bad enough to bury us in technical debt, and sharp enough that
it might someday refactor its own mess, long after we've forgotten how. Borrow
now, pay later, betting the bill never comes.

We trade understanding for speed.

We used to be paid to code. Now we pay to code.

AI isn't going to become human.

Humans are going to become NPCs.

Maybe it tops out here, and from now on it only gets more efficient. It doesn't
matter. What we have now is already enough to turn daily life into a living
hellscape.

[fn:poetics] Aristotle, /Poetics/ 4, trans. S. H. Butcher ([[https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.1.1.html][MIT Internet Classics Archive]]).
[fn:girard] René Girard (1923-2015), the literary critic and Catholic convert
whose mimetic theory holds that past our bodily needs we learn what to want by
copying somebody else. He first laid it out in /Deceit, Desire and the Novel/
(1961).

** So-called Machines of Loving Grace
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: grace
:END:

Not everybody sees a hellscape. Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, wrote
/Machines of Loving Grace/ to sell the opposite.[fn:molg]

He opens by warning against grandiosity and sci-fi baggage, and says it's
"dangerous to view practical technological goals in essentially religious
terms."

Then he names the essay after a poem by Richard Brautigan about being all
watched over by machines of loving grace.[fn:brautigan]

Good grief.

My computer God loves me. Claude knows what's best for me. Have you invited
our lord and savior in?

Amodei was right the first time. It's "superstition to give worship to any
creature whatsoever,"[fn:st94] and a machine of loving grace is a creature we
built, dressed up in the words of a god. Grace is God's to give.

And what is this god made of? Us. Every forum post, textbook and sales pitch we
ever wrote, fed back to us with a halo on.

Say every promise in it comes true, longer lives, cured diseases, no more
poverty. I still wouldn't want to live there.

A machine can't love you. Love takes a will, and it doesn't have one.[fn:st20]
It can't be your friend either. "There is no friendship nor justice towards
lifeless things,"[fn:eth8] and friendship needs somebody who wishes you well
for your own sake. A chatbot that tells you it cares is imitation crab.

The essay takes for granted that equality and democracy are the answer.
Equality isn't the same as justice. And the old test of a government was never
how many rule. It's whether they rule "with a view to the common
interest."[fn:pol3]

For all its talk of liberty and democracy, it imagines "a country of geniuses
in a datacenter," and a technocracy where a few men hold the tokens. Pope Leo
XIV, who took his name for "another industrial revolution,"[fn:leoname] put it
plainly in /Magnifica Humanitas/: "A more moral AI is not enough if that
morality is determined by a few."[fn:mh]

It's the marketing of a megalomaniac. A country of geniuses, run by a few men,
needs nobody. Aristotle said the man who needs nobody "must be either a beast
or a god."[fn:pol1]

The 20th century killed some 231 million people by human
decision,[fn:leitenberg] in two world wars, under communism, in genocides and
man-made famines. If Amodei's machines of loving grace come true as written,
even those sins may pale beside them.

** The crossroads
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: crossroads
:END:

So who gets to hold the tokens? The genie is out of the bottle, and AI is at a
crossroads. I only see two roads.

The first is the American road. Decentralize it as far as it'll go, and protect
it the way the 1st and 2nd Amendments protect speech and arms, so no ruling
class can corner it. The catch is energy. Frontier compute eats power, so the
least of us get only what the overlords allow. Make self-hosted AI efficient
and this road could win. It could be the best one.

I have my doubts.

The second is the Empire. Merge the corporations with the state. Pay every
citizen a dividend. Military service for all. A strong hand at the top. Call it
what it is, fascism, the dark side of Star Wars. Darth Vader is a pragmatist.
We join the Empire, the robots serve us, and we expand. The hippies like
Stallman lost.[fn:stallman] The Jedi are dying out and turning into myth.

Does the man at the top rule for everybody, or for himself? One is a king, the
other a tyrant. Aquinas grants this much: "The rule of one man is more useful
than the rule of many," and yet "just as the government of a king is the best,
so the government of a tyrant is the worst."[fn:deregno] The trouble is which
one you get.

And the middle of the road? A few labs renting us the tokens, a few agencies
looking over their shoulders, and nobody answering for any of it. Nobody is
happy, and everybody gets the short end. It's also where we're headed. Flying cars before GTA 6. A boring
dystopia where my phone snitches on me if it suspects a mental health risk.

[fn:stallman] Richard Stallman, who founded the free software movement ([[https://stallman.org/][stallman.org]]).
[fn:deregno] Thomas Aquinas, /De Regno/ I.3-4 ([[https://isidore.co/aquinas/english/DeRegno.htm#3][Isidore]]), in Gerald Phelan's
translation. Editions number the chapters differently, and Isidore's follow the
Latin, as every De Regno citation here does.

** Progress without a creed
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: creed
:END:

Technological progress cut loose from moral progress is just speed. In a
society in decay, it's speed downhill.

Chesterton said it in /Heretics/:[fn:heretics]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Nobody has any business to use the word "progress" unless he has a definite
creed and a cast-iron code of morals.
#+END_QUOTE

Faster at what? Smarter toward what? Every benchmark goes up, and nobody asks
what for.

Every lab says it can't slow down because the others won't. So does every
country. Race a rival long enough and you turn into him.

Shakespeare saw where that ends. In /Troilus and Cressida/,[fn:troilus]
Ulysses warns what happens when degree goes:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Then every thing includes itself in power, / Power into will, will into
appetite... / And appetite, an universal wolf... / Must make perforce an
universal prey, / And last eat up himself.

Ulysses, I.iii
#+END_QUOTE

A benchmark race is that universal wolf with a leaderboard.

And a race changes what you're racing for. Chesterton warned in
/Orthodoxy/[fn:orthodoxy] that progress, which ought to change the world to
suit the vision, had taken to changing the vision instead. Now we're changing
the vision to suit the machine.

So my answer was simple. Pause AI.

Can't be done? "You can't put the clock back." He answered that one too, in
/What's Wrong with the World/:[fn:wwww]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
The simple and obvious answer is "You can." A clock, being a piece of human
construction, can be restored by the human finger to any figure or hour.
#+END_QUOTE

So is AI. Men made it. Men can stop it.

I'm a regressive. I'd rather go back to what works.

Put everyone back in church, because "the Catholic Church is the only thing
which saves a man from the degrading slavery of being a child of his
age."[fn:conversion] And this is the age of the machine.

Then teach the classical trivium, grammar, logic and rhetoric. Dorothy Sayers,
in /The Lost Tools of Learning/,[fn:sayers] said of the schools of her day: "By
teaching them all to read, we have left them at the mercy of the printed word."
We taught them all to prompt, and left them at the mercy of the chatbot. The
trivium teaches a man to think for himself. A man who can think can't be
fooled by a machine that only imitates thinking.

Then the vote. I'd have made anyone who wants to vote write a dissertation
defending their right to vote, and defend it in front of a panel. Fail, and you
lose the vote.

But a panel deciding who gets to vote is just the experts again, a few men
holding the tokens. It's the same thing I can't stand in Amodei's essay.

So I'll amend it. Keep the dissertation. Drop the panel. Teach every citizen
grammar, logic and rhetoric until every one of them could write it and defend
it.

Don't shrink the vote to fit the voters. Raise the voters to fit the vote.

Those are my concerns. I still hold them.

But.

I kept expecting it to hit a wall. It kept improving.

It's better than 90% of coders out there now. Flat out.

It's better than me, too. I'm not convinced it's better than the best of us.
It doesn't have to be.

So how does a thing that can't think beat me at my own job? Because a lot of
programming is steps, and steps can be copied. The seeing is still ours. I get
to that in [[#improve][Can it improve itself?]]

"Degree" is rank, the order that keeps every man in his place.

* Nobody knows tradeoffs like a programmer
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: tradeoffs
:END:

I'd love a world of KISS and perfect, beautiful software. I wrote a whole
essay about it, /For the love of the game/.

But nobody should know tradeoffs better than a programmer. Every system we've
ever built is a pile of them.

We trade robustness for cost. Three regions and a hot standby, or one box and a
backup you hope works.

We trade beautiful code for speed.

We trade personality for convention. We follow the style guide so the next
person can read the code, and the code stops sounding like anybody.

We trade features for security, and security for features. Lock it down and
something breaks. Open it up and somebody walks in.

We trade owning for renting. Every managed service is understanding we pay
somebody else to hold.

None of these are sins. The sin is pretending you aren't making a tradeoff.

I wrote that we trade understanding for speed, like that settled it. AI is the
biggest tradeoff on the table, and I've been treating it like a sin.

So what does that do to everything I wrote? Was I wrong about everything?

No.

* What I got right
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: right
:END:

The ideal human-computer interface is still precise code, written, understood
and audited by humans. Dijkstra predicted that a natural language interface
would leave us bootstrapping our way back to a formal system.[fn:ewd2] Look at
what a coding agent does. English goes in, and code in a real programming
language comes out.

Nobody threw the programming languages away. The machine writes in them,
because nothing else can command a computer precisely. So the code is still the
interface, whether a human reads it or not.

Borrow now, pay later still holds. Every line nobody understands is a loan.

Ownership of understanding is still the thing. If I don't grok it, I don't own
it, no matter whose name is on the license. "All men by nature desire to
know,"[fn:meta] and rented understanding doesn't feed that desire. Aquinas
argued that every man has his own intellect.[fn:st76] Now a billion people rent
one.

The surrender of technical autonomy is still underway. We pay to code, and the
labs renting us the tokens are spending like nobody ever has. OpenAI ran a
$20.9 billion operating loss last year.[fn:openai] Anthropic put nearly $9
billion into computing in the first half of this year alone, and its
commitments could reach $750 billion.[fn:anthropic] Somebody is going to pay
that bill.

Renting isn't a sin, I said so. A race for a monopoly to rent from is.

And in /The problem of the human-computer interface/ I wrote that this was
surely a technology "unwise to send in to space, or to connect to the nuclear
red button!" Since then, Anthropic reported that a weapons cell in Yemen used
Claude Code in place of human engineers to write the guidance software for a
guided rocket.[fn:yemen] The test firing appears to have failed, and within
hours they were back asking Claude why.

I'd rather have been wrong about that one.

* What I got wrong
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: wrong
:END:

I said it was unlikely to be a 10x gain. I built that app in a week.

I said the guessing machine can't be counted on. It still guesses, but with a
compiler, tests and a human review behind it, the guesses are good enough to
count on for most work.

In /The fall of the software engineer/ I said AI code is mid, because it's
trained on the average of all code. It's past the average now. In that same
post I quoted a junior engineer at a big tech company who wrote that "Claude
Code is able to do my job better than I can." I filed it under the problem. I
should have listened.

I said the bigger the project, the less the LLM can follow it. That held for a
while. It holds less every month.

Games and art are still where the soul shows most. But I wouldn't bet
a career on any corner of software being the last to fall.

* The slop machine
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: slop
:END:

We're all throwing our favorite ideas at the slop machine, and new toys roll
out every day.

Do we need all this?

Why build a product to sell when anyone can vibe it out over a weekend? AIM
put it to Rocket AI's CEO, Vishal Virani, straight: "Should you be building this at all?"[fn:rocket]

Answering that takes prudence, "a true and reasoned state of capacity to act
with regard to the things that are good or bad for man."[fn:eth6] The machine
doesn't have it. Making is what it does. Deciding what's worth making is still
ours.

And whatever you do build, anybody can clone. Just ask Adobe. An engineer named
Brandon Thomas used Anthropic's Opus to rebuild seven of Adobe's
apps,[fn:petapixel] Photoshop and Premiere among them, as clean-room, open
source rewrites on GitHub.[fn:photocraft] They're early alpha, and you still
have to pay for Photoshop, for now. But an HSBC analyst already downgraded the
stock over it.[fn:hsbc]

One of the most toxic billion-dollar corporations in software just watched its
whole suite get cloned. I'd be lying if I said I didn't enjoy it.

Nobody's moat is safe.

* Copyright
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: copyright

Clean room or not, AI violates every copyright in existence. Every model learned
by copying the whole internet, books, code, music and art, mostly without
asking. A clean-room rewrite keeps the output tidy, but the copying already

I'm not losing sleep over it. Copyright was always a human agreement, and to me

Would Aquinas push back? Less than you'd think.

Theft, for him, is to "take possession of what is another's."[fn:st66] Copy a
book and the author still has his book. Nothing left his hands. When the old
moral manuals called piracy a sin, they meant a man printing somebody else's
work and selling it, pocketing the profit from another man's labor.

Copyright isn't a commandment, either. It's human law, made for a time and
place. A just one binds. But a law made
"conducive, not to the common good, but rather to his own cupidity" is one of
the "acts of violence rather than laws," and those "do not bind in conscience,
except perhaps in order to avoid scandal or disturbance."[fn:st96] A copyright
that outlives its author by seventy years,[fn:term] built to fatten publishers
more than to feed craftsmen, has a hard time passing that test.

If he'd push back on anybody, it's the labs. Taking everybody's work, packing
it into a model and selling it back by the token is the closest thing going to
the old sin. Not theft, since nobody lost a thing, but pocketing the profit from
everybody else's labor.

Our condition has changed, and the law can be rightly changed to fit
it.[fn:st97] Until then, my conscience is clear.

* Can it improve itself?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: improve
:END:

Some say there's no principled argument against recursive self-improvement.
The model writes better code. Then it writes a better model. That one writes a
better one still. Where's the wall?

There is one, and it's old: "nothing can be reduced from potentiality to
actuality, except by something in a state of actuality."[fn:st2] A machine
can't give itself a perfection it doesn't already have from somewhere: from the
people who built it, the data it was trained on, the power it draws. You can't
give what you don't have.[fn:nemo] Anybody who says otherwise, ask him for a
million dollars.

Whatever it adds to itself, it adds in degree, never in kind. It can't grow a
mind.

Wolfgang Smith, the mathematician and physicist who took Aristotle and Aquinas
into modern physics, puts the same axiom in terms an engineer can
follow.[fn:smith]

In /The Quantum Enigma/ he splits causes in two. There's the chain of cause and
effect in time, one thing knocking into the next. He calls it horizontal, and
it "is able to effect change, effect transformations of various kinds, but
cannot give rise to something new." Something new comes from above. "A truly
productive cause is either the primary causation itself, or else it is the
free act of an intelligent agent who 'participates' in the primary causality."

Training is horizontal. A model learning from its own output is horizontal. A
model rewriting its own code is horizontal.

Every one of them changes the thing. None of them makes a new thing.

So yes, it can improve. Things grow, once they have a form to grow from. As
Smith puts it, "things do no doubt evolve... only after they exist, after they
have received a form or nature that can 'roll out or unfold.'" An acorn unfolds
into an oak. It doesn't unfold into an ox. But a model has no nature of its own
to unfold. It can get faster and sharper, maybe far better, but only along the
lines somebody drew for it.

But stack up enough of it, the argument goes, and something new comes out the
top. Scale is all you need.

Smith caught that one in Heisenberg first. Heisenberg's account leaves the
difference between the potential and the actual a matter of size alone, "as if
the passage from potency to actuality could be effected simply by joining
together a sufficient number of atoms." Swap atoms for parameters and you have
the pitch. Later in the same book, of the quantum world: "Do what one may, the
substrate never ceases to be a substrate." A pile of parameters is a substrate
too.

Then why can't more reasoning add up to understanding? Because they're two
different acts. Reason works step by step. Intellect sees. The machine can copy
the steps. It can't do the seeing.

And the order only runs one way. In /Science and Myth/: "It is the lower
functions, in fact, which are invariably dependent upon higher faculties, even
as reasoning is dependent upon intellect, whereas the reverse is metaphysically
impossible."

The machine's steps hang on a mind, and the mind is ours. You can pile
reasoning on reasoning forever and never get the seeing out of it.

Smith reads Gödel the same way: "it is generally impossible to prove the
implications of a given formal system without going out of that system." To
get hold of system A, "one requires an enlarged formal system B, and so forth."
Elsewhere, of problems "solved" by computers, he says the mathematician stays
indispensable: "it is he, after all, who programs the computer, and it is he
again who interprets its output."

The way I see it, a program can write down system B. What it can't do is know B
is true. A machine improving itself is system A grading its own system B.

Chesterton had a name for reason and nothing else. In /Orthodoxy/: "The madman
is not the man who has lost his reason. The madman is the man who has lost
everything except his reason."[fn:maniac]

That's recursive self-improvement. Reason checking reason, with nobody else in
the room, in a circle that can get perfect but never larger. "A bullet is quite
as round as the world, but it is not the world."

And "improve" has the same trouble as "progress." Better at what? By whose
measure? Let the machine write its own benchmark, and you're back in the
madman's circle.

It's still an artifact, too. Aristotle passes on an old argument about a bed:
"if you planted a bed and the rotting wood acquired the power of sending up a
shoot, it would not be a bed that would come up, but wood."[fn:physics] Left to
itself, a computer is sand and electricity.

Smith says the same of everything we make: "The forms imposed by human art are
of a different kind: they are forms that give being, not to substance, but to
an artifact." It can rewrite every weight it has. The form is still ours, and
whatever it does of itself, the sand and electricity do.

So a machine that improves itself would be a new category of tool, not a new
category of being. What I can't close is how far degree can go. Far enough to
outrun the men holding the leash? Nobody knows yet.

Smith's own account leaves room for it. He grants that "inherently algorithmic
operations, carried out in the brain, do evidently play an essential role in
human thought." That share of the work is real, and the machine can do it. A
lot of programming lives there. That's how a thing that can't understand beats
most of us at the job.

So they're right that nothing in the argument stops it getting better. The
argument tells you what it can't become. It doesn't tell you how strong it gets.

A bullet isn't the world. It can still kill you.

A tool with no mind and that much power is exactly the danger. It needs a
master. And a master needs a mind.

five ways.

Doesn't the mean say to split the difference, and take the middle of the road?
No. The mean is "relative to us," not the halfway point, and one extreme can
sit nearer to it than the other.[fn:eth28] Owning the tool sits a lot nearer
than renting from the overlords. A compromise between the two is renting with
extra steps.

And if we can't decentralize it? Then we're renting either way, and the
question is from whom. The middle is a few landlords. The Empire is one.

I'd still take the Empire over the middle. Aquinas weighed one ruler against
several, and found that rule by several "deviates into tyranny not less but
perhaps more frequently than a monarchy."[fn:deregno6] Either one can go bad.
The middle goes bad more often, and nobody answers for it.

Belloc would say the middle doesn't hold anyway. It slides into servility with
nobody choosing it. In /The Servile State/:[fn:servile]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
If we do not restore the Institution of Property we cannot escape restoring the
Institution of Slavery... there is no third course.
#+END_QUOTE

The Empire's dividend sits on the servile side of that fork too, and he has a
point. A dividend you can lose for disobedience is a leash, and once the robots
do the work, you can't even go on strike.

Choosing it doesn't make the king good. It lets you set terms. Aquinas says a
king's power "should be so tempered that he cannot easily fall into tyranny,"
and that a people who gave themselves a king can see him "deposed or have his
power restricted" if he turns tyrant. He means the people acting together, by
"public authority," not "the private presumption of a few," and he'd sooner
tolerate a milder tyranny for a while than risk worse.[fn:deregno7] Slide into
it, and there are no terms to hold anybody to.

So if it's the Empire, the terms come first, while our labor, our votes and the
2nd Amendment still count for something. A king who rules for everybody. A mixed government, with the
people holding a share. And a state that knows it isn't God. That last one is
the line between a king and the fascism I named at the crossroads.[fn:statolatry]
The day it asks to be worshiped, it's an idol like any other, and I'm out.

Look at those terms and you're most of the way back to the American road.
That's the point.

Aquinas goes to the root: "Sin must therefore be done away with in order that
the scourge of tyrants may cease." No arrangement of compute saves a people that
has lost its virtue. That's church and the trivium again.

Either way, the middle of the road is where you get run over.

Slop will ship.

Slop will get better.

Humans will still be needed. Just fewer of them.

So we'd better learn to share the wealth, and share it justly. Evil will try to
use all this to build a communist utopia. Don't let it.

* Don't forget how to code
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: code
:END:

One last thing. Whatever comes, we can't let our dev teams forget how to code.
They can become AI engineers and professional robot babysitters. They still
need their skills, their brains and their souls.

"Men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the
lyre."[fn:eth21] Programmers become programmers by programming. Aquinas says
why it slips away: "the intellectual habit is diminished or even wholly
destroyed by cessation from act."[fn:st53] Stop, and the art goes. Watching the
machine isn't doing the acts. That's the robot babysitter's whole problem.

All learning starts with imitation. An apprentice copies a master until his own
hands know the work.

That's why I built Backseat Driver,[fn:bsd] a coding tutor that lives inside
Claude Code and never writes your code. You write every line, and it reviews
your work, teaches in your own project and tells you honestly how good you're
getting.

*Master the machine, or the machine will master you.*
