In March I made a video telling you to "tell your AI-pushing boss to get
wrecked."[fn:artisan]

When Aristotle broke with his own teacher, he called it a duty, "for the sake
of maintaining the truth even to destroy what touches us closely."[fn:truth]
What touches me closely is six essays and a video against leaning on AI to
code, all with my name on them.

AI is new, but the questions it raises are old, so I've gone back to Aristotle
and Aquinas. One question won't leave me alone. How does a thing that can't
think beat me at my own job?

* Where I stood
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: stood
:END:

In March, I saw a philosophical problem. The machine can't have a thought of
its own. It doesn't reason. It imitates reasoning.

And look who it's imitating for. Aristotle called man "the most imitative of
living creatures," the one who "through imitation learns his earliest
lessons."[fn:poetics] It's a gift, and it's how we learn anything at all.

So put the most imitative creature on earth in front of a machine that does
nothing but imitate. Who copies whom? We copy the copy.

René Girard added what Aristotle left out.[fn:girard] We don't only copy each
other's ways. We copy each other's wants. So we're easy to fool, and easier
still to steer.

Most of the time, the imitation passes for the real thing. We've tolerated mediocre software for
decades. It works, ship it.

The machine is bad enough to bury us in technical debt, and sharp enough that
it might someday refactor its own mess, long after we've forgotten how. Borrow
now, pay later, betting the bill never comes. We trade understanding for speed. We used to be
paid to code. Now we pay to code.

AI isn't going to become human. Humans are going to become NPCs.

Maybe it tops out here, and from now on it only gets more efficient. It doesn't
matter. People who copy instead of think, code nobody understands, and a
monthly bill to a lab for the privilege. That's a hellscape, and what we have
now is already enough to build it.

[fn:poetics] Aristotle, /Poetics/ 4, trans. S. H. Butcher ([[https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.1.1.html][MIT Internet Classics Archive]]).
[fn:girard] René Girard (1923-2015), the literary critic and Catholic convert
whose mimetic theory holds that past our bodily needs we learn what to want by
copying somebody else. He first laid it out in /Deceit, Desire and the Novel/
(1961).

** So-called Machines of Loving Grace
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: grace
:END:

Not everybody sees a hellscape. Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, wrote
/Machines of Loving Grace/ to sell the opposite.[fn:molg]
His usual subject is the risks, and his company
researches how to reduce them. This essay is the other half, his sketch of "what
a world with powerful AI might look like if everything goes right."

He opens by warning against AI leaders who talk "like a prophet leading their
people to salvation," and says it's "dangerous to view practical technological
goals in essentially religious terms."

Then he names the essay after a poem by Richard Brautigan about being all
watched over by machines of loving grace.[fn:brautigan] Plenty of readers take
that poem as ironic. Even the poem he borrowed doesn't trust the
caretaker. But there's no irony at the end, where he calls the vision "a thing
of transcendent beauty," one he thinks will leave many
"literally moved to tears." Good grief.

Amodei was right the first time. Divine worship belongs to God alone, so it's
"superstition to give worship to any creature whatsoever,"[fn:st94] and a machine of loving grace is a creature we
built, dressed up in the words of a god.

And what is this god made of? Us. Every forum post, textbook and sales pitch we
ever wrote, fed back to us with a halo on. One cause of
idolatry, Aquinas says, is that "man takes a natural pleasure in representations,"
citing the same chapter of the /Poetics/ I quoted above. Then he quotes Scripture's
craftsman, who carves a tree "like the image of a man," and prays to it
"concerning his substance, and his children, or his marriage."[fn:st94a4] Now we
ask the chatbot.

And "watched over" is his own picture, not mine. An "AI coach" who "studies your
interactions," an idea he calls "very promising." And among the options he
floats, granting they all have "tons of possible problems," AI systems that hand
out resources to humans by "what the AI systems think makes sense to reward in
humans."

He promises real goods too, most cancer gone and the human lifespan doubled.
I'd take the cures. I don't want the caretaker.

A machine can't love you. Love takes a will, and will follows
intellect.[fn:st20] It has neither. He says himself that meaning comes "mostly from human relationships and
connection." Then why call the machines loving?

It can't be your friend either. Friendship is goodwill returned, and you can't
even wish a lifeless thing well, since "it would surely be ridiculous to wish wine
well."[fn:eth8] It has no will to wish you anything back. A chatbot that tells
you it cares is imitation crab.

** Progress without a creed
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: creed
:END:

Technological progress cut loose from moral progress is just speed. In a
society in decay, it's speed downhill.

Chesterton said it in /Heretics/.[fn:heretics]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Nobody has any business to use the word "progress" unless he has a definite
creed and a cast-iron code of morals.
#+END_QUOTE

Faster at what? Smarter toward what? Every lab says it can't slow down because the others won't. So does every
country. Race a rival long enough and you turn into him.

Shakespeare saw where that ends. In /Troilus and Cressida/,[fn:troilus]
Ulysses warns what happens when degree goes.

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Then every thing includes itself in power, / Power into will, will into
appetite... / And appetite, an universal wolf... / Must make perforce an
universal prey, / And last eat up himself.

Ulysses, I.iii
#+END_QUOTE

A benchmark race is that universal wolf with a leaderboard. Chesterton warned in
/Orthodoxy/[fn:orthodoxy] that progress, which ought to change the world to
suit the vision, had taken to changing the vision instead. Now we're changing
the vision to suit the machine.

So can we pause AI? A whole movement wants the United States to lead an
international treaty to do it.[fn:pauseai]

Can't be done? "You can't put the clock back." Chesterton answered that one
too, in /What's Wrong with the World/.[fn:wwww]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
The simple and obvious answer is "You can." A clock, being a piece of human
construction, can be restored by the human finger to any figure or hour.
#+END_QUOTE

So is AI. Men made it. Men can stop it.

So I'm a regressive. I'd rather go back. Put everyone back in church, because "the Catholic Church is the only thing
which saves a man from the degrading slavery of being a child of his
age."[fn:conversion] And this is the age of the machine.

Then teach the classical trivium, grammar, logic and rhetoric. Dorothy Sayers,
in /The Lost Tools of Learning/,[fn:sayers] said of the schools of her day, "By
teaching them all to read, we have left them at the mercy of the printed word."
We taught them all to prompt, and left them at the mercy of the chatbot. Teach
a man to think, and a machine that only imitates thinking can't fool him.

Those are my concerns. I still hold them, even though I've since changed my
mind about the pause.

* Nobody knows tradeoffs like a programmer
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: tradeoffs
:END:

I'd love a world of KISS and perfect, beautiful software. I wrote a whole
essay about it, /For the love of the game/.[fn:j5]

But nobody should know tradeoffs better than a programmer. Every system we've
ever built is a pile of them.

We trade robustness for cost. Three regions and a hot standby, or one box and a
backup you hope works.

We trade beautiful code for speed.

We trade personality for convention. We follow the style guide so the next
person can read the code, and the code stops sounding like anybody.

We trade features for security, and security for features. Lock it down and
something breaks. Open it up and somebody walks in.

We trade owning for renting. Every managed service is understanding we pay
somebody else to hold.

None of these are sins. The sin is pretending you aren't making a tradeoff.

I wrote that we trade understanding for speed, like that settled it. AI is the
biggest tradeoff on the table, and I'd been treating it like a sin. Was I wrong about everything?

No. But I got some of it wrong.

[fn:j5] [[https://enchant.games/journal/2026-03-12-love-for-the-game/][For the love of the game]], Enchant Games journal, March 12, 2026.

* What I got wrong
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: wrong
:END:

I kept expecting it to hit a wall. It kept improving. I said it was unlikely to
be a 10x gain. People told me AI changes everything. They had a point.

I built a whole platform by myself. A library of old public-domain books,
re-typeset. An AI research librarian that answers from that library, with
citations. And a community with the features of Facebook and then some.
Profiles and walls like Facebook. Encrypted messages like Signal. Disappearing
messages like Snapchat. Anonymous accounts. Calls.

Under the hood, it's no toy. A typed front end in PureScript. My own API on
Cloudflare Workers, with a database, file storage and real-time chat, sharded
so it scales. The infrastructure is code too, in Terraform. Every change goes through CI, waits
for my approval before it reaches production, rolls out in stages, and gets
tested against production every night.

It took a week to get it standing.[fn:history]
That's when I knew software engineers were cooked.

I said the guessing machine can't be counted on. It still guesses, but with a
compiler, tests and a human review behind it, the guesses are good enough to
count on for most work.

In /The fall of the software engineer/[fn:j3] I said AI code is mid, because it's
trained on the average of all code. It's past the average now. In the same
post I quoted a junior engineer at a big tech company who wrote that "Claude
Code is able to do my job better than I can." I filed it under the problem. I
should have listened.

I said games and art are where the soul shows most. But I wouldn't bet a career
on any corner of software being the last to fall.

It's better than most coders I've worked with now. Flat out. It's better than
me, too. I'm not convinced it's better than the best of us.
It doesn't have to be.

So how does a thing that can't think beat me at my own job? That's the question
I started with.

* AGI doesn't matter
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: agi
:END:

I still don't believe AGI is possible, if AGI means a machine with a mind of its
own. A machine can't have a mind.

Aristotle said the mind "cannot reasonably be regarded as blended with the body," and has no
organ the way sight has the eye.[fn:deanima] Grasping a universal, what makes a
triangle a triangle and not just this one or that one, isn't something matter
does,[fn:st75] no matter how much of it you wire together.

Why not? Draw a triangle. It's this triangle, this size, these angles, in this
spot on the page. Every triangle made of matter is like that, one particular
triangle. Your idea of a triangle isn't any of them. It fits every triangle
there is, big or small, drawn or not, and it's no size at all. Aquinas's rule
is that "whatever is received into something is received according to the
condition of the recipient." Matter makes things particular, so what matter
takes in, it takes in as one particular. If the mind were matter, "the forms of
things would be received into it as individuals, and so it would only know the
individual."[fn:st75a5]

A computer is the plainest case. Everything in it is a particular, these bits,
these voltages, these weights. A type called Triangle is some bytes in a file,
and it means every triangle only because a programmer reads it that way. A
trained model sorts triangles it has never seen, just as a concept would. That's
the shaping. A machine can be shaped to act as if it understood. It can't
understand.

The functionalist has an answer, and it's a good one. What more is there to
understanding a triangle than handling every triangle you meet, even ones you've
never seen? If the model does that, it has the concept. Asking for anything more
is asking for a ghost.

But a sieve does that. It sorts every stone it will ever meet, stones it has
never seen, by a rule it never breaks, and it grasps nothing. Handling new cases
is behavior. The question is what's doing it, and the model gives itself away.
Add noise too faint for anyone to see to a picture a network calls a panda, and
it calls it a gibbon, with "99.3% confidence."[fn:gibbon] Nobody who grasped
what a panda is gets fooled by noise. It was tracking pixels all along. And if
the functionalist says our neurons are particulars too, so our grasp must be the
same trick, Aquinas agrees with the first half. That's why he says the intellect
has no organ.

Harold Bloom argued that Shakespeare's people become themselves by hearing
themselves think out loud.
"Self-overhearing is their royal road to individuation."[fn:bloom]

A reasoning model does the self-overhearing part. It writes out a chain of
thought and reads it back. Nobody is home to overhear it.

Girard has a line about writers in /A Theater of Envy/ that fits the machine
better than any playwright.[fn:envy]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Writers are such mimes, we are told, that they can feign a thousand states of
mind that they never experience themselves. This is true, no doubt, but it is
not the whole truth... What a genuine writer truly desires to represent is his own
state of mind.
#+END_QUOTE

The machine is all first half, a thousand states of mind and none of its own.
That makes it the perfect model to copy, always sure of itself.

It doesn't matter. Nothing says a thing needs a mind to be powerful. Make it
cheaper, keep it improving, put it in robots, and the world will never
be the same, barring an act of God, like a solar flare that fries every chip on
earth.

[fn:st75] The intellect "has an operation per se apart from the body"
(/Summa Theologiae/ I q.75 a.2, [[https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1075.htm#article2][New Advent]]).

* Can it improve itself?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: improve
:END:

Here's the case for recursive self-improvement, and its believers say nothing
in principle stops it. The model writes better code. Then it writes a better model. That one writes a
better one still. Where's the wall?

There is one, and it's old. Nothing, Aquinas says, "can be reduced from
potentiality to actuality, except by something in a state of actuality."[fn:st2] A machine
can't give itself a perfection it doesn't already have from somewhere, from the
people who built it, the data it was trained on, the power it draws. You can't
give what you don't have.[fn:nemo]

Whatever it adds to itself, it adds in degree, never in kind. It can't grow a
mind.

Mind you, that's metaphysics, not a forecast. It says what the machine is, not
how well it will score.

Wolfgang Smith, the mathematician and physicist who took Aristotle and Aquinas
into modern physics, puts the same axiom in terms an engineer can
follow.[fn:smith] He splits causes in two. There's the chain of cause and
effect in time, one thing knocking into the next. He calls it horizontal, and
it "is able to effect change, effect transformations of various kinds, but
cannot give rise to something new." Something new comes from above. "A truly
productive cause is either the primary causation itself, or else it is the
free act of an intelligent agent who 'participates' in the primary causality."

Training is horizontal. A model learning from its own output is horizontal. A
model rewriting its own code is horizontal. Every one of them changes the thing. None of them makes a new thing.

So yes, it can improve. As Smith puts it, "things do no doubt evolve... only after they exist, after they
have received a form or nature that can 'roll out or unfold.'" An acorn unfolds
into an oak. It doesn't unfold into an ox. But a model has no nature of its own
to unfold. It can get faster and sharper, maybe far better. It can even surprise
the people who built it. Surprise is still degree. Whatever it does, it does as
an artifact, with a form it didn't give itself.

But stack up enough of it, the argument goes, and something new comes out the
top. Scale is all you need. Smith caught the same mistake in Heisenberg, long before AI. Heisenberg's account leaves the
difference between the potential and the actual a matter of size alone, "as if
the passage from potency to actuality could be effected simply by joining
together a sufficient number of atoms." Swap atoms for parameters and you have
the pitch. And of the quantum world, "Do what one may, the
substrate never ceases to be a substrate." A pile of parameters is a substrate
too.

Then why can't more reasoning add up to understanding? Because they're two
different acts. Reason works step by step. Intellect sees.[fn:st79] The machine can copy
the steps. It can't do the seeing.

Seeing isn't a hunch. A lot of what we call a programmer's intuition is habit,
pattern worn in by practice, and the machine copies that too.[fn:st78] The
seeing I mean is narrower. What the thing is for. Whether it's right, not just
whether it runs. Maybe its imitation of that judgment gets good enough too. Even then, somebody
has to answer for it, and only somebody with a mind can.

And the order only runs one way. In /Science and Myth/ he writes, "It is the lower
functions, in fact, which are invariably dependent upon higher faculties, even
as reasoning is dependent upon intellect, whereas the reverse is metaphysically
impossible."

The machine's steps hang on a mind, and the mind is ours. Of problems "solved"
by computers, Smith says the mathematician stays indispensable, since "it is he, after
all, who programs the computer, and it is he again who interprets its output."

Chesterton had a name for reason and nothing else. In /Orthodoxy/ he wrote, "The madman
is not the man who has lost his reason. The madman is the man who has lost
everything except his reason."[fn:maniac]

That's recursive self-improvement. Reason checking reason, with nobody else in
the room. Chesterton says the madman's mind "moves in a perfect but narrow
circle," and "the insane explanation is quite as complete as the sane one, but
it is not so large. A bullet is quite as round as the world, but it is not the
world." Perfect isn't the same as big. The machine can polish its circle forever
and never grow into a new kind of thing.

It's still an artifact, too. Aristotle passes on an old argument about a bed, that
"if you planted a bed and the rotting wood acquired the power of sending up a
shoot, it would not be a bed that would come up, but wood."[fn:physics] Left to
itself, a computer is sand and electricity.

Smith says the same of everything we make. "The forms imposed by human art are
of a different kind: they are forms that give being, not to substance, but to
an artifact." It can rewrite every weight it has. The form is still ours.

What I can't close is how far degree can go. Far enough to outrun the men holding the leash? Nobody knows yet.

Smith's own account leaves room for it. He grants that "inherently algorithmic
operations, carried out in the brain, do evidently play an essential role in
human thought." That share of the work is real, and the machine can do it. A
lot of programming lives there. That's how a thing that can't think beats me at
my own job.

In March I said it can't think, so it can't really win. The believers say it
keeps getting smarter, so a mind will come. Each of us had half of it. I was
right about the mind and wrong about the power. They're right about the power
and wrong about the mind.

No mind, and dangerous all the same. A bullet isn't the world. It can still
kill you.

* Even Linus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: linus
:END:

I use it, as a tool. Even Linus Torvalds does. Tom's Hardware reported on a
patch series he built with an LLM.[fn:toms]

It started as a branch to make reading files from the page cache, the copy of
your files the kernel keeps in memory, scale. His
write-up of the branch is plain about how it was made.[fn:commit]

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
The code, the test programs, the review behind the list above and the first
draft of this text were done with an LLM coding assistant (Claude Code) under
my direction. The measurements were run on my machine.
#+END_QUOTE

The write-up owns up to ten known problems. Another kernel developer, Kiryl
Shutsemau, found the first version could get processes killed when a machine
ran short of memory, and had a better idea, to hold back only the pages that get
read again and again.

Linus reworked it, with two patches from Kiryl, and posted it to the mailing
list[fn:lore] as rcu-free-v3.[fn:rcu] He called his own best benchmark, 23
times faster, "not a real number in the sense that no real load will ever see
it."

Kiryl found a race too, a read that could return bytes nobody ever wrote
there.[fn:race] None of it has merged. That isn't the method failing. That's
peer review doing its job.

Most of us aren't Linus. That's what tests, review and staged rollouts are for.
They hold what one head can't.[fn:whiteboard]

That's what using it well looks like. The machine wrote the code. A man who
knows the kernel as well as anyone alive directed it, measured it, put it in
front of his peers, took their correction, and owns the result.

A tool acts only as the hand moves it, so the work is the hand's.[fn:st62] When
Linus signs his patch, he owns it. Each one carries an "Assisted-by: LLM" line,
right next to his "Signed-off-by." When a company ships slop, it can't blame the
model.

It's the interface I argued for back in March, precise code understood and
audited by humans, with a much faster pair of hands.

Girard would catch me here. Am I doing it because Linus does? That would be
copying his wants. It was my own build that changed my mind. What I take from
Linus is how he uses it.

* The right fear
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: dark-side
:END:

So I'm changing. I'm embracing the dark side. That's how it looked from where I stood in March. Using the machine was the
dark side, and the pragmatists were Darth Vader. Take the power that's on the
table and put it to work. The free software hippies like Stallman
lost.[fn:stallman]

My living depends on it, but that's a reason I weigh, not an order I follow.
The clock can go back, remember.
Chesterton warned in /The Outline of Sanity/ that "those who begin by saying
that we cannot abolish the machine, that we must use the machine, are
themselves refusing to use the mind."[fn:outline] I'm choosing it, with my
mind, knowing what it costs.

PauseAI US asks people to sign [[https://www.pauseai-us.org/petition/][a petition]] for "a global AI Treaty to ban
superintelligent AI systems until they can be made safe." In March I'd have
signed it. Would I still? No. Men can stop it, but can isn't should.
Prudence weighs what a pause would give up, and I'd take
the cures. It also asks whether the means can work, and a treaty that can't be
verified binds nobody.[fn:st49] Pope Leo XIV asks for a politics "capable of
slowing things down when everything is accelerating."[fn:mh] I respect the
bishop of Rome without holding his every judgment binding, and on this one I
part ways with him.

But I pay for an AI subscription, and the company behind it is racing the others
to the next model. Doesn't that feed the wolf? The evil
isn't building the thing. It's the racing, the creedless wolf Ulysses warned
about. My fee can't pay for the building without paying for the race
too. Money is fungible. But what I buy is a tool, and the racing is their
choice, not mine. The moral theologians have a name for it, cooperation. Sharing in
the evil, wanting what the other man wants, is formal cooperation, and it's
never allowed. Helping without wanting it, from far off, is remote material
cooperation, and it's allowed for a proportionate reason.[fn:coop] I don't want
the race, a monthly fee is a long way from it, and my livelihood is a
proportionate reason. If using the tool ever meant wanting the race, I'd
be out.

There's one more condition, scandal, which means not leading anyone else into
sin by your example. Writing this makes me an example, so here are the terms,
and I don't always meet them. I use it the way Linus does. I keep the craft. I'd rather run a model on hardware I own than
rent one, once the open models are both good and efficient enough.

Chesterton would take the tool, then ask whose tool it is. His fight was over who owns the means of making things. "Too much
capitalism does not mean too many capitalists, but too few
capitalists."[fn:divorce] A monthly bill to a lab is too few capitalists, the
owning for renting trade at its worst.

He even had a case like ours. In his day it was electricity. A central plant
was "a real dependence," he granted, but "if electricity could be cheapened,
the chances of a very large number of small independent shops, especially
workshops, would be greatly improved." So use it, he decided, "to break up the
hopeless block of concentrated capital and management." The point was never the
machine. "We are concerned to produce a particular sort of men, the sort of men
who will not worship machines even if they use machines."[fn:sanity]

The models are the electricity this time. Friends of mine already run open
models on hardware they own. One of them runs four agents at once on a single
graphics card. The models aren't the frontier yet, but they do real work. Make
them cheaper still, and a town could own a datacenter the way it owns its
library, a shop could own its model, and the common
man gets his workshop back.

America's founders protected a machine once already. The 1st Amendment guards "the
freedom of speech, or of the press,"[fn:first] and the press was a machine. England licensed
its presses until 1695, and the founders made sure Congress never could.
Nobody needs a license to run one. A model on your own hardware is the press of our day, and
nobody should need a license to run that either. The law can only keep the door
open. If we walk through it and own the thing, the power stays with the people.
Who's to say we don't get there?

That's where Stallman ended up. He has a name for renting what you
could run yourself, Service as a Software Substitute, and says it gives "someone
else power over your computing."[fn:saass] The free software hippies lost the
war, and here I am at their conclusion, by Chesterton's road. Stallman would tell me to
cancel my subscription. He'd have a point.

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Fear is the path to the dark side... fear leads to anger... anger leads to
hate... hate leads to suffering.

Yoda, /Star Wars: Episode I/[fn:yoda]
#+END_QUOTE

The joke's on me. I walked every step of the path Yoda warned about, resisting.

First I feared using it. Then I feared being forced to use it. Then I feared
falling behind if I didn't. Then I feared being replaced by it.

Then the anger. I told you to tell your AI-pushing boss to get wrecked.

Then the hate. I hated falling behind. I had disdain for anyone who'd trust a
random guessing machine, a magic black box, a slop generator.

Hate leads to suffering, and I've had my fill. The way off that path is virtue,
which is "a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us."[fn:eth2] Between the zealot
and the luddite stands the brave man, the one "who faces and who fears the
right things and from the right motive, in the right way."[fn:eth3]

Courage gets you through the fear. It doesn't make the call. That's prudence,
which Aquinas calls the "right reason of things to be done."[fn:st57] Choosing
it with my mind means choosing it by prudence.

The machine grows in degree, never in kind. It can't become a mind, but it can
become powerful. Power without a mind needs a master, and a master needs a mind.
If it isn't yours, it'll be the lab's, or nobody's at all.

Fear of falling behind isn't the right fear. Fear of being mastered is.

And being mastered won't look like a tyrant. It'll look like Amodei's
caretaker, a coach that studies you and a system that decides what to reward in
you. The machine of loving grace is the master with a smile.

I called the machine the dark side. Fear was the dark side,
and I was already there. What I'm embracing is a tool, used with a mind.

* Don't forget how to code
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: code
:END:

Whatever comes, we can't let our dev teams forget how to code. They still need
their skills, their brains and their souls.

"Men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the
lyre."[fn:eth21] Programmers become programmers by programming. Aquinas says
why it slips away, that "the intellectual habit is diminished or even wholly
destroyed by cessation from act."[fn:st53] Watching the machine isn't doing the
acts.

Aquinas sets prudence beside its twin. Art is the "right reason of things to be
made."[fn:st57art] Coding is an art. Whether and how to use the machine is prudence. Losing
the art isn't a sin in itself. Neglecting it when other people depend on what
you understand can be.

The hellscape I warned about is still possible. Linus shows how not to build it.
The machine writes, and a man who still knows how to code understands, measures
and answers for it.

*Master the machine, or the machine will master you.*
